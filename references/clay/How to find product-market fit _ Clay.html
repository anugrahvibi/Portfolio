How to find product-market fit

Product-market fit means the people you built for keep using your product and pay for it. This playbook covers how to get there in three steps, with a tool that finds your first users and a coach for when you get stuck.

Find your first users
  1. Pick who you build for

Step 1: Pick who you build for

Decide who your product is for before you build it. That group is your ideal customer profile, or ICP: the kind of company and person who has the problem you want to solve, and has it the worst. Even if you’ve already built something, routinely revisiting your ICP helps you prevent drift and stay focused on your most important customers.

It sounds obvious, yet startups still skip it. CB Insights read 111 startup post-mortems, and 35% blamed "no market need." Only running out of money came up more. "No market need" usually means the founders built for people who didn't have the problem, or didn't have it badly enough.

So pick one group. Describe your ICP by what you can see from the outside: industry, company size, location, and the roles on their team. For example, "recruiting agencies in the US with 10 to 50 people and no full-time scheduler." If you can spot them from the outside, you can go find more of them.

What makes a good first user

The tool gives you a list of people who match your ICP. Your first users come from that list, and some are better to start with than others. A good first user has a problem big enough that they've already tried to fix it on their own. Before you reach out to anyone on your shortlist, get clear on three things.

Their pain
What's the problem, in their words? What does it cost them each week in time, money, or stress?
Your fix
What do they stop doing once they have your product? What can they do that they couldn't before?
The fit
If the pain is big and your fix clearly removes it, people keep using it and pay for it. That's product-market fit. If not, change who you build for or what you build.

Talk to 20 to 40 people before you build

Their pain, your fix, and the fit are all educated guesses until you've actually talked to people. So is your ICP. Talk to 20 to 40 before you build. The more the better. What you don’t want is to talk to so few people that any single person’s opinion sways how you address the market.

Split the conversations with purpose. More than half should be with people who match your ICP. The rest should be just outside it: a different industry, a company twice the size, or another country. They should still be people who could use what you're building. To find them, change who it's for in our ICP tool and run it again.

In every conversation, ask about the pain and what they've already tried.

Then compare the two groups. If the people who match your ICP want it more, your guess was right. If the outside group wants it more, they're your ICP now or should be incorporated into it. Redraw it around them. "Want it" means they ask when they can use it, or offer to pay. "Sounds cool" doesn't really carry much weight.

Step 2: Test your ideas

Step 1 was about who to build for. This step is about what to build for them.

Your first idea is a guess too. To test it, put the smallest version that works in front of the people from Step 1 and watch what they do with it. A prototype works for this. So does a clickable mockup, or a service you run by hand.

Once you see what they do, expect to change course. Each company below started with one idea, learned something, and changed direction before it found product-market fit. Seven of the nine took more than a year, so if it takes you a while, don’t feel like you’re falling behind.
